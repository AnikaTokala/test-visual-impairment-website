The Accessibility Problem of Math Screen Readers

Learn more about the challenges with screen readers, and how we can face them.

The Problem

Trying to solve a math equation while someone is reading symbols aloud with no pauses is difficult. They could mispronounce operators... read too quickly... and even skip important parts of the equation entirely. A fraction once easily understood becomes much harder to figure out. Is it a mixed fraction? Regular? Is it part of the numerator or denominator?

Symbols designed to carry meaning lose their purpose when we only have sound to go off of. For many visually impaired students, this is what mathematics becomes when their primary translator is the average screen reader.

While modern screen readers have significantly improved accessibility for reading plain reading languages, they continue to struggle with mathematics because math is a visual language that depends heavily on symbols and organization. As education becomes increasingly digital, inaccurate math screen readers create serious barriers that limit equal access to learning for people with visual disabilities.

What Are Screen Readers?

Screen readers are assistive technologies designed to read text out loud. Popular screen readers include JAWS, NVDA, and VoiceOver.

High school students can access many of these screen readers for free. NVDA is free, while VoiceOver comes pre-installed on iOS devices. Limited access to text-to-speech tools like TTSReader and NaturalReader are also available online for free.

These technologies can help read articles, explore websites, and do assignments. However, mathematics presents a special challenge because equations rely on visual placement and symbolic meaning rather than simple left-to-right reading.

Our Testing

To better understand these limitations, my partner and I tested online screen readers and the Java Speech API using mathematical symbols. The tests measured whether software correctly pronounced symbols and preserved their original meaning.

Several major errors appeared. The factorial expression !n was read without acknowledging the exclamation mark, changing the operation entirely. The transpose symbol T was interpreted as a normal letter rather than a mathematical operation. Symbols such as ≫ and ˜ were not recognized at all.

Other symbols, including arrows and logical notation, were spoken literally instead of mathematically.

Why Symbol Errors Matter

These mistakes may seem small, but one incorrect symbol can completely change the meaning of an equation.

Reading a plus sign as a minus sign reverses an operation. Misreading mV (millivolt) as MV (megavolt) changes a value by a factor of one million.

Structural and relational errors are particularly serious because they can change the logic of an equation. Unlike ordinary reading mistakes, mathematical errors cannot always be inferred from context. One missing exponent or operator may create a completely different answer.

Interview Findings

Interviews also gave insight into the experiences visually impaired people have with accessibility tools.

One visually impaired person explained that she relies on TV magnifiers, large text, and accessibility settings. Math becomes overwhelming because equations and symbols are difficult to process quickly.

Switching between devices and taking notes interrupts concentration, and many screen-reading voices sound robotic, often reading unnecessary toolbar information instead of important content.

Her experience demonstrates that accessibility is not only about reading words aloud but also about meaningful interpretation.

Students With Dyscalculia

This issue is especially important for students with dyscalculia and other learning disabilities. Dyscalculia affects a person’s ability to understand numbers, calculations, and mathematical relationships.

If accessibility software skips or misreads symbols, it may increase confusion and frustration. Anxiety and lack of confidence can worsen when tools designed to help instead create more barriers.

More reliable accessibility tools could reduce learning obstacles by presenting mathematical equations more clearly.

Conclusion

As schools continue shifting toward digital learning, accessible mathematics technology is becoming a necessity.

Current screen readers work well for reading text, but mathematics still presents many weaknesses. Improving these systems requires better standards for mathematical speech, stronger testing methods, and accessibility-first design.

Equal access to education means mathematics can be learned by anyone, regardless of how they receive information.
